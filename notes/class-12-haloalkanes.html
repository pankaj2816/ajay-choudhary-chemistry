Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

SN1 vs SN2, elimination, and why haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution.

2 min read

The big idea

In R–X, the halogen pulls electrons, leaving the carbon slightly positive. A nucleophile is attracted to that carbon and pushes the halogen out. Whether this happens in one step or two decides everything (SN2 vs SN1).

SN2 vs SN1

SN2SN1
Stepsone (concerted)two (carbocation forms first)
Rate lawrate = k[RX][Nu⁻] (second order)rate = k[RX] (first order)
Best substratemethyl > 1° > 2° (less crowding)3° > 2° (more stable carbocation)
Stereochemistryinversion (Walden inversion)racemisation (mixture)
Favoured bystrong nucleophile, polar aprotic solventweak nucleophile, polar protic solvent

Reactivity of R–X: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F, because the C–I bond is weakest.

Elimination

With alcoholic KOH, haloalkanes lose HX to form alkenes (β-elimination). Saytzeff's rule: the more substituted alkene is the major product.

  • Aqueous KOH → substitution (alcohol). Alcoholic KOH → elimination (alkene).

Important reactions

  • KCN gives nitriles (R–CN); AgCN gives isocyanides (R–NC). CN⁻ is ambidentate.
  • Wurtz: 2RX + 2Na → R–R. Fittig / Wurtz–Fittig: aryl versions.
  • Grignard reagent: RX + Mg (dry ether) → RMgX, a very useful carbon nucleophile.

Why haloarenes are less reactive

In chlorobenzene the C–Cl bond gets partial double-bond character from resonance, the carbon is sp² (holds electrons tighter), and the phenyl cation is unstable. So nucleophilic substitution needs harsh conditions, unless electron-withdrawing groups (−NO₂) sit at ortho/para positions.

Tip: Chlorine on benzene is deactivating but ortho/para directing: −I effect slows the ring down, +R effect decides the position.

Quick revision

  • Chirality: a carbon with four different groups; optical activity is measured with a polarimeter.
  • DDT and CFCs are environmental concerns (persistence, ozone depletion).
  • Chloroform is stored in dark bottles because light converts it to poisonous phosgene.
