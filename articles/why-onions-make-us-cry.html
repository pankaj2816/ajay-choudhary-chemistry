Why do onions make us cry?

The tears are a chemical defence. Cutting an onion starts a quick chain of reactions that ends with a gas that irritates your eyes.

By Ajay Choudhary Sir · 19 November 2026

An uncut onion does not make you cry. The trouble begins the moment the knife breaks its cells.

A defence that starts when cells break

Onion cells store sulfur-containing compounds and enzymes in separate compartments. Cutting the onion breaks the cells, mixes them, and starts a fast chain of reactions:

  1. An enzyme called alliinase breaks the sulfur compounds into sulfenic acids.
  2. A second enzyme, lachrymatory factor synthase, converts them into a volatile gas: syn-propanethial-S-oxide.
  3. This gas floats up into your eyes, irritates the nerves, and your eyes make tears to wash it away.

This is a natural defence that discourages animals from eating the onion.

Tricks that really work, and why

  • Chill the onion first: at low temperature, the gas evaporates more slowly and the enzymes work more slowly.
  • Use a sharp knife: fewer cells are crushed, so less of the gas is made.
  • Cut near running air or under a fan: the gas is carried away before it reaches your eyes.
  • Leave the root end for last: it has a higher concentration of the sulfur compounds.

Connect it to your syllabus

Enzymes as biological catalysts (Class 12, Biomolecules); effect of temperature on reaction rate (Class 12, Chemical Kinetics).
